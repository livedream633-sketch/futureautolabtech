kW vs kWh: The EV Charging Terms Everyone Mixes Up

By Bilal Ahmad Alvi — CEOUpdated October 2, 202610 min read

If you have read anything about electric cars, you have seen two similar-looking units: kW and kWh. They sound alike, they are printed side by side on spec sheets, and they are confused constantly. The good news is that the difference is simple once you attach each to a picture. This guide explains both, gives you the one formula you need for charging time, and shows why the biggest number on a charger is not always the one that matters.

kWh is the size of the tank

A kilowatt-hour (kWh) is a quantity of energy. It is what a battery stores and what you pay for on an electricity bill. A car with a 60 kWh battery can hold 60 kilowatt-hours of energy, in the same way a fuel tank holds a certain number of gallons. Efficiency then tells you how far that energy goes. If a car uses about 0.3 kWh per mile, which is about 3.3 miles per kWh, a full 60 kWh battery is good for roughly 200 miles, before allowing for weather and driving style.

kW is how fast energy flows

A kilowatt (kW) is a rate of power: how quickly energy is moving. A charger rated at 11 kW delivers up to 11 kilowatt-hours of energy in one hour. Think of it as the width of the hose filling the tank. A wide hose fills faster, but the tank is still the same size. Home wall outlets deliver a couple of kW, home Level 2 chargers commonly deliver 7 to 11 kW, and public DC fast chargers range from about 50 kW to 350 kW.

The one formula to remember

Charging time is roughly the energy you need divided by the power you can actually receive: time (hours) ≈ kWh needed ÷ kW. It ignores small losses, but it is close enough for planning.

Worked example: 20% to 80% on a 60 kWh battery

Going from 20% to 80% adds 60% of the pack, which is 36 kWh. At 7 kW that takes about 5.1 hours. At 11 kW it takes about 3.3 hours. On a DC fast charger able to deliver 150 kW for the whole session it would take about 14 minutes. In real life it takes longer, for reasons covered next. At a home electricity price of $0.16 per kWh, those 36 kWh cost about $5.76 before charging losses, which are commonly around 10%. Why the peak kW number can mislead

A charger advertised at 150 kW does not deliver 150 kW from start to finish. Batteries accept power along a charging curve: fast in the middle of the range, then slower as the battery fills, and slower again if the pack is cold. That is why fast-charging stops are usually quoted as 10% to 80%, and why the last 20% can take as long as the first 60%. A car that holds a moderate power for longer can beat a car with a higher peak that drops off quickly, so look at average charging speed, not just the headline figure.

The car has a limit too

Your car decides how much power it will take. For AC charging, an onboard charger converts the power, and many cars cap it at 7 to 11 kW. Plugging into a 22 kW public AC station does not make that car charge faster than its own limit. DC fast charging bypasses the onboard charger, but the car and battery still set the maximum. Check both the station’s rating and your car’s maximum AC and DC charge rate.

Where you will see each unit

Battery capacity, energy used on a trip and the price on your bill are all in kWh. Charger ratings, charging speed and motor output are in kW. If a number describes an amount, it is kWh. If it describes a rate, it is kW. For home setups, read our guide to Level 1 vs Level 2 charging, and use the EV savings calculator to turn kWh into running costs.

kW and kWh on your electricity bill

Your household electricity bill is based on kilowatt-hours. If the car takes 36 kWh from the grid and your price is $0.16 per kWh, the energy costs about $5.76, no matter whether it took five hours or fifteen minutes. The speed of charging (kW) changes how long you wait, not how much energy you pay for. Some commercial customers also pay a “demand charge” based on their highest kW draw, but ordinary home accounts are normally billed on kWh only, sometimes at different prices at different times of day.

This is why charging slowly overnight and charging fast on a trip can cost very different amounts per kWh even though the energy is the same: the price comes from the supplier and the type of station, not from the unit itself.

Efficiency: miles per kWh and kWh per 100 miles

Efficiency links the two worlds of energy and distance. You will see it written two ways. “Miles per kWh” is how far the car travels on one kilowatt-hour, so higher is better. “kWh per 100 miles” is how much energy it takes to go 100 miles, so lower is better. A car using 30 kWh per 100 miles travels about 3.3 miles per kWh. Both are the same measure, but the second makes it easy to see running costs: at $0.16 per kWh, 30 kWh costs $4.80 per 100 miles.

The same car will not always match its sticker value. Speed, cold weather, heavy loads, hills and strong headwinds all raise consumption. Use the official figure to compare cars with each other and your own trip data to predict what you will actually see.

Quick reference: turning kW into miles per hour

You can convert a charger’s power into the range it adds per hour with one multiplication: kW × miles per kWh. A 7.2 kW charger and a car that does 3.3 miles per kWh add roughly 24 miles of range per hour. A 1.4 kW Level 1 connection adds about 4.6 miles per hour for the same car. A 150 kW DC fast charger could add about 495 miles of range per hour at full power, which sounds enormous but only applies for part of a session because the charge rate tapers. A few other conversions are handy:

  • →1 kW = 1,000 watts. 1 kWh = 1 kW sustained for one hour.
  • →Energy (kWh) = power (kW) × time (hours).
  • →Cost = kWh used × price per kWh, plus losses of around 10% when charging.
  • →Range = kWh in the battery × miles per kWh.

Why a bigger battery is not always better

It is tempting to assume that more kWh is always the better choice, but a larger battery brings trade-offs. It adds weight and cost, takes longer to fill from a slow home charger, and may give you range you rarely use. A 90 kWh battery on a 7 kW home charger needs a long night to go from low to full. If your daily driving is short and you charge overnight, a smaller battery that you top up every day can serve you just as well and cost less. If you often take long trips without easy charging, extra capacity and a fast charging curve are worth paying for. Choose the battery for the trips you really make.

Bottom line

Remember the tank and the hose: kWh is how much, kW is how fast. Divide one by the other for charging time, expect real sessions to run longer than the ideal figure, and compare cars by their average charging speed rather than peak power alone.
