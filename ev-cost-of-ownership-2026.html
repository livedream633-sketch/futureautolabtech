True Cost of Owning an EV in 2026 vs a Gas Car

By Bilal Ahmad Alvi — CEOUpdated October 2, 202612 min read

Sticker price is only the start. The real cost of owning a car is fuel or electricity, insurance, maintenance, tires, registration, financing and — usually the biggest item — depreciation. This guide shows how to compare an EV with a gas car properly, using a transparent example you can rebuild with your own numbers.

A note on incentives

The federal EV tax credits ended for vehicles acquired after September 30, 2025, so this comparison assumes no federal credit. Some states and utilities still offer rebates or cheaper charging rates; add only the ones you have verified. See our EV Incentives Checker for where to look.

Step 1: Energy cost per mile

Illustrative assumptions (change them for your situation): electricity at $0.17/kWh, an EV that uses about 1 kWh per 3.5 miles, a gas car rated 30 mpg, gasoline at $3.20/gallon and 12,000 miles per year.

  • →EV, charging at home: $0.17 ÷ 3.5 ≈ $0.049 per mile → about $583 per year.
  • →Gas car: $3.20 ÷ 30 ≈ $0.107 per mile → about $1,280 per year.
  • →Yearly saving: about $697, or roughly $3,486 over five years.
  • →EV, using public DC fast charging at $0.40–$0.60/kWh: about $0.114–$0.171 per mile — similar to or higher than gas.

That last line is the key lesson: EV savings depend heavily on where you charge. Drivers with home charging save the most; drivers who rely on fast chargers may save little.

Step 2: The other costs

  • →Maintenance: EVs skip oil changes and rely less on brake pads thanks to regenerative braking, but tires can wear faster because of vehicle weight and instant torque.
  • →Insurance: often higher for EVs because repair costs can be higher. Get quotes for the exact models you are comparing.
  • →Depreciation: can swing thousands of dollars either way and differs by model, price cuts and battery health. Check recent resale values.
  • →Home charger: a Level 2 charger and installation commonly costs a few hundred to a couple thousand dollars depending on your electrical panel and wiring — see our home charging guide.
  • →Registration and fees: some states add an annual EV fee, and rules on sales tax differ.

Step 3: Add it up over five years

Total cost of ownership = purchase price + financing + energy + insurance + maintenance + fees − expected resale value − verified incentives. Do this for both cars over the same period and mileage. If the EV costs more up front, the fuel savings determine the break-even point — use our break-even calculator or the full 5-year TCO calculator.

Depreciation: the cost most buyers underestimate

Depreciation is the difference between what you pay for a car and what it is worth when you sell it. For many new vehicles it is the largest single cost of the first five years, often bigger than fuel and maintenance combined. Electric cars have had a mixed record: some models lost value quickly after price cuts or when newer, longer-range versions arrived, while others held their value well. Because the market keeps changing, a general rule is not much help. Look at recent listings and sold prices for the exact model, trim and age you are considering, and compare them with the original sticker price.

Two things make EV depreciation harder to predict. First, battery and charging technology are still moving, so a used buyer may pay less for an older model with slower charging or a different connector. Second, manufacturer discounts and incentives change what new cars cost, and used prices tend to follow. A practical approach is to run your comparison twice: once with an optimistic resale value and once with a cautious one. If the EV still comes out ahead in the cautious case, your decision is robust.

A five-year example, line by line

Using the energy assumptions from Step 1 (12,000 miles a year, so 60,000 miles over five years), the energy bill alone works out as follows:

  • →EV charged at home: about $0.0486 per mile × 60,000 ≈ $2,914.
  • →Gas car at 30 mpg and $3.20 per gallon: about $0.1067 per mile × 60,000 ≈ $6,400.
  • →EV that relies mostly on public DC fast charging at $0.40–$0.60/kWh: about $6,857–$10,286.

On energy alone, the home-charged EV is about $3,486 cheaper over five years, which matches the yearly saving shown earlier. Now add the items that do not depend on how you fuel the car. Here is a purely hypothetical case with round numbers: the EV costs $4,000 more than the comparable gas car, you spend $1,200 on a home charger, and insurance is $300 a year higher ($1,500 over five years). Those extras add up to $6,700 against energy savings of $3,486, so the EV would still be roughly $3,200 behind before counting maintenance and resale value.

That is not a prediction; it only shows why fuel savings alone cannot settle the question. If the EV is priced the same as the gas car, or you qualify for a verified state or utility rebate, the same energy savings leave you clearly ahead. Replace every number with a quote you have actually received.

Common mistakes when comparing costs

  • →Comparing different cars. A fully loaded EV against a base-trim gas car makes the EV look worse; the reverse makes it look better. Match size, features and performance.
  • →Assuming all charging happens at home. If you cannot charge at home, price your real mix of public chargers instead.
  • →Treating today’s gasoline price as permanent. Test a higher and a lower price and see whether the answer changes.
  • →Ignoring financing. Compare the actual monthly payment and total interest on both offers, not just the sticker prices.
  • →Counting an incentive you have not confirmed. Only include rebates you have verified in writing, including their income and price limits.
  • →Forgetting the charger. Add the cost of the home charger and any electrical work to the EV side.

Cheaper electricity with time-of-use rates

Many utilities offer a time-of-use plan with a lower price overnight. If your car is plugged in by evening and charges after midnight, your effective rate may be well below the $0.17/kWh used in the example, which widens the EV’s advantage. The reverse can also happen: on a plan where evening electricity is expensive, charging the moment you arrive home can cost more than you expect.

Check your utility’s rate schedule, and use the scheduling feature in your car or charger to move charging into the cheap window. Some utilities also offer a dedicated EV rate or a separate meter, so ask what is available before you decide what a mile will really cost.

How to use these numbers

Write down the inputs you can actually verify: purchase price after confirmed incentives, your electricity rate, the local gasoline price, annual miles, insurance quotes for both cars and a cautious resale estimate. Enter them into the break-even calculator, then change one input at a time to see which assumption matters most.

If the result flips when you change your mileage by a few thousand miles or the electricity rate by a few cents, treat it as a close call and decide on other factors such as comfort, range needs and whether you can charge at home. If the EV wins under most reasonable assumptions, you can be more confident. Either way, you will know which number deserves a second look before you sign.

Who saves the most with an EV?

  • →Drivers who can charge at home overnight, especially on a time-of-use rate.
  • →People who drive 10,000+ miles a year, where fuel savings add up.
  • →Buyers who keep the car for many years, spreading depreciation.
  • →Households with cheap electricity and expensive local gasoline.

📌 Key Takeaway

With home charging and typical assumptions, an EV can save several hundred dollars a year in energy costs, but the answer changes with electricity prices, mileage, insurance and depreciation. Always run your own numbers before deciding.
