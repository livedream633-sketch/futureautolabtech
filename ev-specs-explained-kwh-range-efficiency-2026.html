EV Specs Explained: kWh, kW, Range and Efficiency

By Bilal Ahmad Alvi — CEOPublished September 30, 20269 min read

Key takeaways

  • kWh is energy (the size of the tank). kW is power (how fast energy flows). Mixing them up is the most common spec-sheet mistake.
  • Efficiency (miles per kWh) matters as much as battery size: range is roughly battery size multiplied by efficiency.
  • For charging, compare the 10–80% time, not the peak kW number in the brochure.
  • Treat EPA range as a comparison tool. Your real range will vary with speed, temperature, tires and load.

EV spec sheets are full of numbers with similar-looking units: kW, kWh, mi/kWh, MPGe, volts. If you are shopping for your first electric car, it is easy to focus on the biggest number and miss the one that matters. This guide explains each spec in plain language, with worked examples, so you can compare cars on what will affect your daily life.

kW versus kWh: power and energy

These two units are the foundation of every EV spec, and they measure different things.

  • kilowatt-hour (kWh) measures energy, like the size of a fuel tank. A 75 kWh battery stores 75 kilowatt-hours.
  • kilowatt (kW) measures power, like the rate at which fuel flows through a pump. A 150 kW charger delivers 150 kilowatts of power.

A helpful picture is a water tank and a hose. The kWh figure is how much water the tank holds. The kW figure is how fast water flows through the hose. A bigger tank does not fill faster, and a faster hose does not make the tank bigger.

Energy equals power multiplied by time. A 10 kW charger running for one hour delivers 10 kWh. That single relationship lets you estimate almost any charging time.

Battery capacity: gross versus usable

Manufacturers may quote gross capacity (everything the pack holds) or usable capacity (what the car lets you actually use). The car keeps a buffer at the top and bottom to protect the battery. Usable capacity is the number that decides your range, so look for it in the specifications or on the EPA listing. A pack quoted at 78 kWh gross might offer about 75 kWh usable.

Efficiency: how far each kWh takes you

Efficiency tells you how much energy the car uses per mile. It is expressed in three common ways:

  • Miles per kWh (mi/kWh). Higher is better. Many modern sedans manage roughly 3.5 to 4 or more, while large SUVs and trucks are often around 2.5 to 3.
  • kWh per 100 miles. Lower is better. It is the inverse of the above, similar to how gas cars once used gallons per 100 miles.
  • MPGe. The EPA’s way of comparing EVs with gas cars, based on 33.7 kWh being the energy content of one gallon of gasoline.

MPGe is handy for a rough comparison, but for costs, mi/kWh is more useful because you pay for electricity by the kWh. At $0.17 per kWh, 3.5 mi/kWh costs about 4.9 cents per mile. Our cost of ownership guide uses the same assumptions.

Range: capacity multiplied by efficiency

Range is roughly usable battery capacity multiplied by efficiency. That is why a car with a bigger battery is not automatically better: an inefficient large SUV with a huge pack may go no farther than a light sedan with a smaller one.

The example shows why real-world range moves around. The same battery can give around 300 miles when driven gently in mild weather at 4.0 mi/kWh, and only about 190 miles at 2.5 mi/kWh, which is what fast highway driving in freezing weather can look like. Our winter range guide has planning figures for cold weather.

What is EPA range?

EPA range comes from standardized lab tests, so you can compare cars fairly. It is not a promise. Your result depends on speed, temperature, heating and cooling, tire size, terrain, load and driving style. Use EPA range to compare models, then apply a sensible allowance for your own conditions.

Charging specs that matter

AC charging (home and public Level 2)

AC charging speed is limited by the car’s onboard charger, often 7 to 11 kW. A 48-amp home charger cannot charge faster than the car will accept. For a 75 kWh battery, going from 10% to 80% means adding 52.5 kWh. At 9.6 kW that takes about 5.5 hours before losses, so plan on roughly 6 hours. See Level 1 vs Level 2 charging for how to choose.

DC fast charging

Fast charging is where brochures can mislead. The headline figure is peak power, the maximum the car can accept at its best moment, usually at a low state of charge. Power then drops as the battery fills. What matters for a road trip is how long the car takes to go from 10% to 80%.

Take the same 52.5 kWh (10% to 80% of a 75 kWh battery). A car that averages 105 kW across that window needs 30 minutes. A car with a higher peak but a steeper fall-off might average only 70 kW and need 45 minutes. Peak kW alone would not have told you that. For planning a long drive, see our road trip guide.

Voltage: 400 V and 800 V

Some newer EVs use an 800-volt architecture instead of the older 400 volts. Higher voltage can support higher charging power at lower current, but it is not a guarantee of a faster stop. The 10–80% time on a real charger is the honest measure.

Common spec-sheet mistakes

  • Assuming a bigger battery is always better. It costs more, weighs more and takes longer to charge on AC.
  • Comparing peak kW. Compare 10–80% time instead.
  • Confusing range with efficiency. A long range on a huge battery can hide poor efficiency, which raises your running cost.
  • Ignoring your own driving. If you drive 30 miles a day and charge at home, 250 miles of range is plenty and a long-range premium may be wasted.
  • Trusting one number. Look at range, efficiency, charging time and warranty together.

A quick checklist for comparing two EVs

  1. Note usable battery size in kWh.
  2. Note EPA range and efficiency (mi/kWh).
  3. Check AC charging power and 10–80% DC time.
  4. Read the battery warranty, including the capacity floor.
  5. Estimate running cost using your electricity price and the efficiency figure.
  6. Use our EV Savings Calculator to see the difference against your current car.

Bottom line

Once you know that kWh is energy, kW is power and efficiency links them to miles, a spec sheet stops being intimidating. Focus on usable capacity, efficiency, real charging time and warranty, and ignore the single biggest number in the brochure. Then match those figures to how you actually drive and charge.
