EV Battery Degradation: How Fast Do Batteries Really Age?

By Bilal Ahmad Alvi — CEOUpdated September 30, 20269 min read

Every rechargeable battery loses some capacity over time, including the one in your phone and the one in an EV. The question buyers really want answered is how fast, and whether it is something to worry about. The reassuring news from most data is that modern EV batteries age slowly. This guide explains what degradation is, what affects it and how to slow it down.

Key takeaways

  • Degradation is the gradual loss of capacity: a 300-mile car now going 285 miles has lost roughly 5%.
  • A typical pack may retain around 90% after five years, though results differ by car and use.
  • Gentle charging habits and avoiding long periods at 100% or near empty help.
  • Read the battery warranty, including any capacity-loss terms.

What degradation actually means

Degradation is the gradual loss of a battery’s usable capacity. If a car was rated for 300 miles when new and can now go about 285 miles under the same conditions, it has lost roughly 5% of its capacity. The loss does not usually cause sudden failure. It shows up as a slow drop in range. Most cars also keep a hidden buffer at the top and bottom of the battery’s range, which helps protect the cells and can make early aging less visible.

How much loss is typical?

Studies of large groups of real-world EVs have generally found average capacity loss in the range of a couple of percent per year, with many vehicles showing faster loss in the first year or two and then a slower, steadier decline. That means a car might have around 90% of its original capacity after five years, though results differ by car and use. Treat these as averages, not guarantees, and remember your climate and driving matter.

What speeds up wear

Heat. High temperatures are the biggest long-term enemy of lithium-ion cells. Cars parked in very hot climates and charged at high temperatures tend to age faster. Cars with good thermal management, usually liquid cooling and heating, handle heat better.

Time at very high or very low charge. Leaving the battery at 100% for long periods, or letting it sit near empty, adds stress. Charging to 100% for a road trip and driving soon after is a different matter from parking at 100% for weeks.

Frequent DC fast charging. Fast charging pushes high current through the cells and produces heat. Doing it occasionally is fine. Doing it every day as the main way of charging can add wear over many years.

High mileage and deep cycling. Batteries wear with use, so a high-mileage car generally shows more degradation than a low-mileage one of the same age.

Habits that help

Use Level 2 home charging for daily needs when you can, since it is gentle and cheap. If the manufacturer recommends it for daily use, set a charge limit around 80–90%, and charge to 100% only when you need the range. Avoid leaving the car at very low charge for long periods. Park in shade or a garage in hot weather. Use fast charging when you need it on trips, without worrying about it. Keep the software up to date, since manufacturers sometimes improve battery management through updates. See our Level 1 vs Level 2 guide for home charging basics.

How to check the health of a battery

Some cars show a battery health figure in a menu or app. Others need a dealer or third-party diagnostic tool. Compare the displayed full-charge range with the original rated range under similar conditions, remembering that temperature and driving style affect the displayed range. If you are buying used, ask for a state-of-health report and read our used EV checklist.

Warranty basics

Most manufacturers back the battery with a warranty, commonly around eight years and a set mileage limit, and some specify a minimum capacity, such as a percentage of original, below which they will repair or replace it. Terms vary, cover usually applies to defects and excessive loss rather than normal wear, and coverage can differ for a second owner. Read the warranty document for your specific car and keep service records.

What does a replacement cost?

Replacing a full battery pack can be expensive, which is why the warranty matters. But most cars never need a full replacement in normal use, and repairs are increasingly done at module level. If you are worried, get a quote from an EV-qualified service center for your model and ask whether independent repair options exist.

The future

Battery chemistry and management keep improving, and newer designs aim for longer life. Our overview of battery technology in 2026 covers what is real and what is still in the lab.

Bottom line

Battery aging is real but usually gradual. Keep the car out of extreme heat, avoid long periods at 100% or near empty, use fast charging as needed rather than daily, and check the warranty before you buy. For most drivers the battery will outlast the years they plan to own the car.
