NACS vs CCS: What the EV Charging Plug Change Means for You

By Bilal Ahmad Alvi — CEOUpdated September 30, 20269 min read

For years, EV drivers in North America had to think about plugs: one shape for most non-Tesla cars, another for Tesla. That is now changing as many automakers move to the connector Tesla developed. If you are shopping for an EV, or already own one, it helps to understand what is happening and what, if anything, you need to do. This guide explains the two connectors in plain language.

Key takeaways

  • NACS is a compact plug for AC and DC charging, standardized by SAE as J3400.
  • CCS cars keep working, and many makers approve adapters for NACS stations.
  • Check the port of any EV you plan to buy, new or used.
  • Home charging is not affected by the change.

What are CCS and NACS?

CCS stands for Combined Charging System. In North America, the version used on most non-Tesla EVs is often called CCS1. Its plug has a round upper part for AC charging and two extra pins below for DC fast charging, which makes it fairly large.

NACS stands for North American Charging Standard. It was designed by Tesla and uses one compact, slim plug for both AC and DC charging. Tesla opened the design to others, and it was later standardized by SAE International as J3400. In everyday use you will hear it called NACS or the Tesla plug.

Why the industry is moving to NACS

Several reasons drove the shift. NACS is smaller and lighter, which many drivers find easier to handle. Tesla’s Supercharger network is large, and drivers of other brands gained access to it as manufacturers reached agreements with Tesla. And a single common plug is simpler for everyone than two competing ones. Many automakers announced they would add NACS ports to new models and provide adapters for existing ones, so the change is gradual rather than overnight.

What it means depending on your situation

You own a Tesla. Your car already uses NACS. Adapters are available that let you use many CCS stations, and it is worth checking with Tesla about the adapter options for your model and any speed limits.

You own a CCS car. Nothing breaks. You can keep using CCS fast chargers, and many manufacturers offer or approve an adapter that lets you use NACS stations. Always use the adapter approved for your model, because power limits and features can differ.

You are buying a new EV. Check which port the specific model and model year has. Some models have already switched to NACS, others use CCS with an adapter option, and some are in transition. Ask the dealer to confirm in writing which connector the car has and whether an adapter comes with it.

You are buying a used EV. Confirm the port type, ask whether an adapter is included, and check that the car can access the networks you plan to use. Our used EV checklist covers this and other checks.

How adapters work

An adapter is a passive or active piece of hardware that connects one plug shape to a different port. It does not change how fast the car can charge: the car, the charger and the adapter together set the limit. Some cars also need a software update or an activation step before they can start a session at a different network. If you rely on adapters, keep the manufacturer’s approved one in the car, keep it dry and clean, and store it where it will not get damaged.

Home charging is not affected

This debate is mostly about public fast charging. For home charging, Level 1 and Level 2 charging use the same standard connector for most non-Tesla cars in North America, and Tesla’s home equipment uses its own plug. If you plan to buy a home charger, choose one that matches your car’s port or ask the manufacturer about approved options. See our Level 1 vs Level 2 guide for how to size home charging.

Practical tips for the transition

Check your car maker’s official charging page for the current list of compatible networks and adapters. Use the manufacturer’s app or your in-car navigation, which often shows which stations your car can use. Do not buy cheap, uncertified adapters, since a poor connection can overheat. And keep a backup plan on road trips, because station availability, pricing and access rules change quickly. Networks and agreements are still evolving, so verify the details for your exact car before you rely on them.

What about outside North America?

The picture is different in other regions. Europe uses the CCS2 connector as its standard, and other regions use different systems such as CHAdeMO or GB/T. Everything in this guide applies mainly to the United States and Canada.

Bottom line

NACS versus CCS is a transition, not a crisis. If you are buying, confirm the port and adapter. If you already own a car, keep using what you have and use the manufacturer-approved adapter if you want to reach more stations. To plan how long a stop will take, try our charging time calculator.
